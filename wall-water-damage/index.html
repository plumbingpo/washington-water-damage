Wall and Drywall Water Damage Help in Washington

Think of drywall as a wick. Paper and gypsum pull water up from the floor line, so the wet zone frequently ends a foot or more past the highest point the water itself touched. From the room, the surface may seem dry and sound even though the hidden face and the insulation are saturated. Looks cannot be trusted here, so every wall gets tested with a meter.

A wet wall does not automatically mean demolition. Firm panels wetted by clean water are frequently saved, in some cases with the baseboard taken off and small openings drilled so air can move inside the wall. If the drywall has gone soft, crumbles, shows mold or took in dirty water, we do a flood cut, meaning a level cut over the damaged area with all material beneath it removed.

Warning Signs

  • A musty smell that is stronger near one wall or outlet
  • Fasteners and joint lines becoming visible on a once smooth wall
  • Speckled growth showing up where furniture sits against the wall or at corners
  • Soft or crumbly spots you notice when pushing lightly on the wall
  • Discolored lower drywall with a visible line marking how high water wicked
  • One wall noticeably cooler under your hand than the rest
What You Cannot See

What Hides Behind It

Hidden Behind Cabinets

Wet drywall behind cabinets, vanities and built-ins gets no airflow and is easy to overlook. Meter readings at the toe kick and from the far side of the wall reveal it.

Mold Inside Cavities

Paper lining, darkness and stagnant air make the hidden side of a wall a perfect place for mold. That side may be coated in growth even as the room side appears spotless.

Soaked Batts in the Wall

Fiberglass batts soak up water and hold it against the studs. A wall with wet insulation will not dry from the room side alone.

Wet Sheathing Outside

Outside walls have sheathing past the insulation that can also get soaked. Because it is slow to dry, we test it before any new drywall goes up.

How the Damage Progresses

First hours

Water Starts Climbing

Moisture enters where the panel meets the floor and wicks upward from there. Both the baseboard and the bottom plate start absorbing water.

24 to 48 hours

Swelling and Blisters

Moisture keeps climbing, the baseboards puff up and blisters form in the paint. Growth is able to get going on the paper that lines the hidden side.

Days 3 through 7

Panels Lose Strength

The gypsum core turns soft and its paper starts to separate. Within the wall the insulation sags, a smell develops and mold moves over the back of the panel.

After a week

Framing Is Affected

Studs and plates stay wet long enough to grow mold and begin to decay. Much more wall now has to be removed and rebuilt.

How We Fix It

How We Handle Wall and Drywall Water Damage

1

Map the Moisture

Every wall is checked with a thermal camera and moisture meter, and we mark the height and reach of the water, covering the other face and the rooms next door.

2

Decide What Stays

You get a straight answer on what can be dried where it is and what must be removed, judged by the category of water, the time it sat and the firmness of the drywall.

3

Cut or Ventilate

Where the drywall stays, the trim is pulled and airflow is sent inside the wall. Walls that cannot be kept get a flood cut along a level line, with the wet insulation pulled.

4

Drying and Reconstruction

Air movers and dehumidifiers run until studs and remaining drywall read dry, typically 3 to 5 days. The rebuild follows with fresh insulation, drywall installation and finishing, trim and a coat of paint.
