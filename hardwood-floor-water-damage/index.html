Hardwood Floor Water Damage: What Washington Homeowners Should Know

It does not take long for water to change a wood floor. As the wood absorbs water along its edges and bottom face, it expands, crowds the neighboring boards and cups, leaving the center lower than the sides. A floor in this condition is not necessarily ruined. When drying starts within the first few days, solid hardwood frequently lies flat again. Success depends on a slow, even drying rate that draws water from the flooring and from the subfloor beneath it.

We dry hardwood with floor drying mats. Each mat is sealed against the floor and hooked to a vacuum unit, which pulls moisture upward through seams and boards as dehumidifiers dry the air in the room. Compared with carpet or drywall it is slower, commonly a week or more. Sanding has to wait until the wood is fully dry. If cupped boards are sanded while still wet, crowning shows up down the road.

Signs to Look For

  • Boards lifting off the subfloor or tenting upward at the seams
  • Board centers humped higher than the edges, called crowning
  • Fasteners working loose, heard as squeaks where the floor was soaked
  • Nail heads and seams ringed by dark staining
  • Swollen trim along the edge of the floor near the leak
  • A musty smell rising from the floor with nothing visible on top

The Clock

How the Damage Progresses

First hours

Water Goes Under

Most of the water ends up beneath the boards, having run down through the joints. You can towel off the top and still have a saturated underside.

Hours 24 to 48

Edges Begin to Rise

Boards swell and the edges rise. A cloudy look can appear in the finish. The bare underside of the boards and the subfloor are where mold can start.

Day 3 to 7

Pressure Builds Up

Expanding boards push harder and harder on one another. Edges get crushed together, nails work loose and parts of the floor may start rising from the subfloor.

After a week

Permanent Damage

Expect a good chance of mold below, dark staining and boards that have buckled. By now replacement, not drying, is the answer for most of the affected section.

What We Do

What Happens After You Call

1

Measure the Moisture

We take readings on the boards, the subfloor and unaffected flooring nearby for comparison. From those numbers we learn the depth of the water and if the floor can realistically be saved.

2

Extract Standing Water

Rugs and furnishings come off the affected boards while pooled water is vacuumed up at once. The faster water stops feeding the seams, the less the boards swell.

3

Install the Mat System

We tape drying mats to the floor and attach them to a vacuum system, which draws moisture upward through the wood. Dehumidifiers control the room so the wood dries evenly without cracking.

4

Monitor and Advise

We track readings every day or two until boards and subfloor match dry reference areas. Our final advice is one of three outcomes, no further work, swapping a few boards, or refinishing the floor.

Behind the Surface

What Hides Behind It

Drying Too Fast

When the top of the wood dries much quicker than the inside, stress builds in the board. The result can be cracks, splits and permanent gaps between boards.

Growth Beneath the Floor

The space between hardwood and subfloor is dark and has no airflow. Mold in that layer cannot be seen from the room, and a musty odor is usually the first sign.

Moisture Under the Boards

The subfloor can hold moisture long after the boards feel dry. Leave it wet and the cupping returns or mold grows up from below.

Stains From Nails and Staples

Staples and flooring nails corrode when moisture lingers, and their grip weakens. The rust also bleeds into the wood as dark stains that sanding may not remove.
