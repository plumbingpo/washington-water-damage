Sump Pump Failure Cleanup in Washington: What to Know

Sump pumps do a single task, yet they commonly quit right when that task matters. Heavy rain raises the water table, the pit fills, and then the power goes out, the float sticks or the pump cannot keep up. The pit overflows and the basement floor starts to flood. Nothing stops it until some pump is working again.

Clean is the wrong word for what comes out of a sump pit. Having passed through the ground, it usually holds bacteria, silt and drain tile residue, which makes it Category 2 at best, or Category 3 once it has been standing or sewage has mixed in. Cleanup is different as a result, with wet drywall, carpet pad and other porous materials generally removed and the rest cleaned and disinfected ahead of drying.

First Five Minutes

Your First Steps

  1. Keep Out of the BasementWith the electricity still on, a flooded basement is not safe to walk into. Outlets, the pump cord or the furnace might already be wet and live.
  2. Turn Off the Basement CircuitsTurn off the basement circuits at the breaker panel, but only if you can reach it from a dry spot. Call the utility when the panel itself sits in the flooded area.
  3. Check the Pump SafelySee if the plug pulled out, the outlet has tripped or the float got stuck on the side of the pit. Reaching into the pit or the water is not safe.
  4. Treat the Water as ContaminatedChildren and pets stay on the upper floor, anyone who has to enter wears rubber boots and gloves, and hands get washed after contact with anything wet.
  5. Move Valuables and CallCarry dry valuables upstairs, photograph the water level and the pump, then call us. Holding off until the basement empties is not the answer.
What We Do

What Happens After You Call

Our crew arrives with extractors and pumps, which means your dead sump is not needed. Low spots, the area by the pit and the rest of the floor are cleared of water and silt.

Common Causes

Why This Happens

  • No backup battery to keep the pump going once storm winds knock out power.
  • A tripped outlet or breaker, or a plug knocked loose, leaving the pump without power.
  • An intake screen packed with mud and stones so the pump cannot draw water.
  • Stuck or snagged float switches, which never tell the pump to start.
  • An old motor that burned out after years of cycling on and off.
If It Is Left

Where the Damage Spreads

Basement Floor Coverings

Groundwater-soaked carpet and padding come out, as cleaning cannot make them safe. Silty water also gets caught below vinyl plank and laminate, which normally means lifting them.

Wall Framing

Bottom plates rest directly on concrete, so they are the first framing to get wet. With the wall opened up, this wood is scrubbed, treated and then dried to normal readings.

Stored Belongings

Contaminated water is absorbed fast by fabrics, books, cardboard and the legs of furniture. Porous items left sitting in the water resist disinfecting and grow mold in a matter of days.

Finished Basement Walls

Dirty water climbs from the slab into insulation and drywall, far higher than the line you can see. The lower part that got wet is cut away, not dried, on account of the contamination.

Will Insurance Pay?

Insurance and Sump Pump Failure Cleanup

Sump pump losses are widely misunderstood when it comes to insurance. A standard homeowners policy usually excludes water that backs up or overflows from a sump, and flood insurance is a separate policy with its own rules. Coverage often exists only if you added a water backup or sump overflow endorsement, and those carry their own limits. Your declarations page will list it, or your agent can tell you. Our records of the failed pump, how high the water rose and what it damaged go into your claim file.

What Affects the Cost

  • The time that passed before the water was pumped out and drying started.
  • Water depth, along with the square footage that flooded.
  • How many stored belongings have to be moved, cleaned or thrown away.
