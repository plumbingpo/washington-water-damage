The Science of Structural Drying in Washington, DC

Home / Blog / Understanding Professional Structural Drying in Washington, DC

Ask people around Washington, DC what restoration involves and drying the structure rarely comes up, although it is the core of the work. Once the visible water is gone, a lot is still held in the building itself, in framing lumber, drywall, insulation, subflooring, and concrete. Mold, lingering smells, and deteriorating materials are the result when the trapped water is not taken out correctly. Here we cover how professional drying works and the reasons it outperforms a do-it-yourself effort by a wide margin.

Psychrometry: The Science Behind Drying

The field behind professional drying is called psychrometry, which examines how mixtures of gas and vapor behave physically and thermodynamically. For a drying job, that comes down to knowing how relative humidity, airflow, and temperature combine to move moisture out of a wet structure. Every job is different, so technicians with IICRC certification calculate the setup psychrometrically, from air mover placement and target humidity and temperature to the number and kind of dehumidifiers.

Drying Equipment Built for the Job

The equipment used for professional structural drying operates on an entirely different scale than consumer products. A residential dehumidifier pulls 2 to 3 pints of water from the air each hour, while an LGR (Low-Grain Refrigerant) unit can pull 15 to 30 or more. Evaporation is sped up by air movers that deliver a directed, high-velocity stream of 2,500 CFM or more across wet surfaces. Under extreme moisture loads or in cold weather, the specialized tool is a desiccant dehumidifier. This equipment represents tens of thousands of dollars in investment and requires specific training to deploy effectively.

Each Stage of Professional Drying

Professional structural drying follows a systematic process. The opening step is a full assessment: thermal imaging and meters locate and map each wet area. From that map come the baseline readings and the plan for where equipment goes. The crew then sets air movers and dehumidifiers where the psychrometric math says they will evaporate and capture the most moisture. Temperature may be raised to increase evaporation rates. Daily visits follow, with readings at each monitoring point, equipment adjustments where needed, and a record of how drying is going. The job is not finished until every wet material tests at an acceptable level against a dry reference standard.

How Many Days Will Drying Take?

Expect 3 to 5 days for the majority of residential jobs when proper commercial gear is used. How long it takes comes down to how much moisture there is, which materials got wet (drywall dries far faster than concrete), how the building is constructed, the ambient conditions, and how many machines of what type are running. Wood framing typically dries in 3 to 4 days, standard drywall in 2 to 3 days, and concrete in 5 to 7 days or more. You will get a projected finish date from the initial readings, and it is revised as the structure dries.

The Reason We Monitor Every Day

Without a daily check, drying is only a guess. Only calibrated readings can tell you if a material is drying at all, if it is on pace, and if it has reached a safe moisture level. Deep in a material, moisture can remain at a dangerous level even though the surface feels dry when touched. Only calibrated moisture meters can provide this verification. There is a paperwork benefit too: documented drying proves to an insurance company that the job met industry protocol, and that backs up your claim and your home's value.
