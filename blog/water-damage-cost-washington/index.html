How Much Does Water Damage Cost in Washington?

Home / Blog / How Much Does Water Damage Cost in Washington?

The Short Answer

In Washington, the usual water damage bill lands in a range of $1,500 to $10,000 or more. With clean water and a small area, the total can stay below about $1,500. At the other end, heavy flooding or sewage with reconstruction work can go well beyond $10,000.

None of this is a quote, only the usual range across the country. Local labor rates and material prices in Washington, DC move the numbers up or down. For a precise figure, there is no substitute for moisture readings taken during an on-site visit.

How Water Type Affects Cost

The first thing a restoration estimate looks at is what kind of water it is. Contamination level places every loss in one of three industry categories. The dirtier the water, the more protective gear, cleaning and material removal the job needs.

Depending on which category applies, US cleanup costs are usually in the area of $3 to $7.50 a square foot. The table below shows common rates.

CategoryWhat it isTypical cost per sq ft
Category 1, clean waterSupply lines, faucets, water heater tanksRoughly $3 to $4
Category 2, gray waterFailed sump pumps, discharge from a dishwasher or washing machineAbout $4 to $6.50
Category 3, black waterSewage, river flooding, storm surgeRoughly $7 to $7.50

The category is not fixed. Leave clean water standing a couple of days and bacteria grow, so it gets handled as black or gray water. Putting things off raises the price for this reason, among others.

What Drives the Price

Two homes with the same broken pipe can get very different bills. Here is what a contractor looks at when pricing a job.

  • Area affected. A larger wet area takes extra crew hours, additional machines and a longer stay.
  • Water category. Clean water is the cheapest to handle. Nothing costs more to deal with than sewage.
  • How long it sat. Water spreads into subfloors and wall cavities over time. A leak found in an hour costs far less than one found after a weekend away.
  • Materials. Concrete and tile are easy to dry. Insulation, drywall, carpet pad and hardwood frequently have to be replaced or dried with special methods.
  • Mold. Once mold is growing, the scope expands to include remediation, along with air filtration and containment.
  • Reconstruction. Drying is one cost. Replacing drywall, flooring, cabinets and paint afterward is a separate one, and on large jobs it is often the bigger of the two.

Most estimates are built around two phases: water extraction first, then structural drying. Plan on 3 to 5 days for the drying in most cases.

Air movers and dehumidifiers are often billed by the day. Three days of drying is cheaper than six.

Cost by Common Job

These are typical national ranges for the jobs we see most often in Washington. They are a place to begin, and an actual price will differ.

  • Burst pipe. Drying and cleanup frequently total $1,500 to $5,000, and the burst pipe repair is on top of that. Expect more if it ran for hours or soaked more than one floor.
  • Appliance leak. A washing machine, dishwasher or water heater leak caught early is often $1,000 to $4,000. Slow leaks under cabinets cost more because of hidden damage.
  • Basement flood. Typically $2,000 to $10,000 or more, depending on depth, the water source and whether the space is finished. Basement water removal in a finished basement sits at the high end.
  • Sewage backup. Many jobs cost $3,000 to $10,000, some more. Because sewage cleanup falls under Category 3, crews disinfect the whole area and take out porous materials.
  • Roof leak. Interior work on wet ceilings, walls and insulation often comes to $1,000 to $5,000, and the roof fix is extra.

Will Insurance Pay for Water Damage in Washington?

Two things decide it: the cause of the loss and your policy's wording. Standard homeowners policies often cover damage that is sudden and accidental, such as a pipe that bursts or a supply line that fails without warning.

Gradual leaks are usually not covered. If a fitting dripped for months, an insurer may call it a maintenance issue. Unless you have a separate flood policy, water that enters from outside, like storm surge or a rising river, is usually excluded. For sewer backup, a dedicated add-on is often required.

Do not guess: look at your policy documents or ask the agent who sold them. Your deductible also matters. A small repair may come in under the deductible, in which case filing may not be worthwhile.

Ways to Lower the Cost

How much you pay for water damage is largely settled within a day or two. Some basic actions go a long way.

  • Act fast. Water keeps spreading until it is removed. More material gets wet with each passing hour.
  • Shut off the water. Learn the location of the main valve ahead of time, and turn it off the moment a leak shows up.
  • Document everything. Shoot video and pictures first, while everything is still in place and nothing has been tossed. Save every receipt.
  • Call a pro before mold starts. Within 24 to 48 hours, mold growth can get underway. After that point, expect added time and added cost.
  • Move what you can. If you can do it safely, lift boxes, furniture and rugs clear of the wet floor.

If the power has not been cut, never wade into standing water, and avoid any water that could carry sewage. Saving money is never worth an infection or an electric shock. Water that entered from outdoors calls for professional flood cleanup by people wearing the correct protection.

Getting a Real Number

A national range helps with budgeting, yet no house is the average house. An inspection with a moisture meter shows how far the water travelled and what it will take to dry and repair.

Damage only gets bigger with time, so call for restoration of water damage in Washington, DC and have the inspection done now.
